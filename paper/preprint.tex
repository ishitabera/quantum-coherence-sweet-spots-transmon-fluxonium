\section*{\color{scipostdeepblue}{Abstract}}
{\bfseries\boldmath
At fixed quality factor, dielectric loss makes a lower-frequency transmon-like qubit relax more
slowly. This has a stronger consequence for design studies than is usually
acknowledged. Under a common noise model, $\log T_2$ over the transmon $(E_J,E_C)$ plane correlates
with $\log f_{01}$ at $r=-0.9999$ within the transmon regime, and over the fluxonium $(E_J,E_L)$
plane at $r=-0.9878$. Because modeled $T_2$ on each plane is largely a function of the transition
frequency, a raw $T_2$ maximum primarily identifies the lowest-frequency corner of the swept domain
rather than an optimal design. Both our initial headline optima lie at grid edges outside the usual bands.

Controlling for frequency changes these conclusions. For the transmon, we find no interior optimum
in $E_J/E_C$ at fixed $f_{01}$: $T_2$ varies by only 3.2\% over $E_J/E_C=11$--$118$, while
the anharmonicity decreases by a factor of 7.3. Charge insensitivity is therefore bought with
anharmonicity rather than coherence, and any reported optimum depends on the imposed regime boundary.

Within this model, the fluxonium has an interior optimum once second-order $1/f$ flux dephasing is
included, but at only 5.4--30\,MHz, a convention-dependent location, where $\bar n_{\mathrm{th}}=10$--$57$ requires active initialization.
Across architectures there is no single answer: the fluxonium/transmon ratio spans ${\sim}400\times$
across the metrics we consider, and at matched frequency the transmon is ahead, with a median $T_2$
ratio of $0.37\times$ at 1\,GHz under our noise priors. A lab-frame pulse simulation puts the
coherence-to-gate-time ratio at a model-dependent $17$--$27\times$, between the two heuristic estimates, partly because the fluxonium drive couples $10.2\times$ more strongly to its leakage transition than to its
qubit transition. Critical-current noise, read as fractional rather than as \texttt{scqubits}
applies it, dominates the fluxonium.\par}

\section{Introduction}

Superconducting qubit design is routinely discussed in terms of \emph{sweet spots}:
bias points at which the transition frequency is stationary with respect to a noise
coordinate, so that first-order dephasing vanishes. The transmon has charge sweet spots
at integer and half-integer offset charge, and its exponentially suppressed charge dispersion
keeps it nearly insensitive in between~\cite{koch2007}; the fluxonium achieves it at
half a flux quantum, where $\partial\omega_{01}/\partial\Phi = 0$ by
symmetry~\cite{nguyen2019}. A natural extension carries the idea from bias to the circuit parameters themselves: the
point of best modeled $T_2$ over a swept parameter plane is read as an optimal design, a
coherence sweet spot in \emph{design} space. The term is ours, introduced to name that reading.

This paper argues that this extension does not survive scrutiny: under the noise models
studied, it systematically selects for low transition frequency rather than for a design. We address three questions:

\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{Q1}] Where are the coherence optima over the transmon $(E_J, E_C)$ plane, and over
      the fluxonium $(E_J, E_L)$ plane, selected $(E_C, E_L)$ slices and flux-bias sweeps?
\item[\textbf{Q2}] Which noise channel dominates where?
\item[\textbf{Q3}] Does the fluxonium's flux sweet spot buy more robustness than the
      transmon's charge insensitivity, and at what cost?
\end{itemize}

Our central methodological claim is that Q1 and Q3 cannot be answered without controlling
for the transition frequency, and that once it is controlled the answers our model gives to both change sign or
dissolve. Our central negative result is that at matched frequency the fluxonium has no coherence advantage
in this model: the transmon is ahead at every matched frequency we test, and in 95\% of noise
draws.

Figure~\ref{fig:summary} summarizes the study. All results are model-based estimates under
literature noise amplitudes. They are not predictions of device performance, and
Section~\ref{sec:calibration} quantifies how far they sit from measurement.

\section{Related Work and Contribution}

\subsection{What is already established}

The frequency scaling on which we build is not a new idea. Dielectric loss at surfaces and
interfaces, commonly attributed to two-level-system defects, is a major relaxation mechanism in
superconducting qubits; in 3D transmons the relaxation rate scales approximately with the
surface participation of lossy dielectrics~\cite{wang2015}. For a weakly anharmonic qubit at fixed
effective quality factor and low temperature ($hf_{01}\gg k_BT$), $\Gamma_1\approx\omega_{01}/Q$:
the charge matrix element, the charging energy and the transition frequency are tied together by
the harmonic-oscillator relations, so a lower-frequency qubit relaxes more slowly. The
fluxonium's matrix elements do not follow those relations. At finite temperature upward
transitions add a further factor
$\coth(hf_{01}/2k_BT)$, which is $1.00$ at \SI{4.92}{\giga\hertz} but $1.91$ at
\SI{0.363}{\giga\hertz} and $8.4$ at \SI{75}{\mega\hertz} at \SI{15}{\milli\kelvin}, so the
simple law fails in the low-frequency fluxonium regime. Design-space optimization under explicit noise models
is likewise established: Papi\v{c} \emph{et al.} identify optimal transmon parameter ranges using
comprehensive noise modeling that includes charge-parity effects~\cite{papic2024}, while Ram
\emph{et al.} present a staged framework spanning circuit design, spectral analysis, and
calibration for both architectures~\cite{ram2025}. Experimentally, fluxonium coherence has
advanced well beyond the devices against which we calibrate our model: Somoroff \emph{et al.}
report $T_2^* = 1.48$\,ms together with an average gate fidelity of
$0.99991$~\cite{somoroff2023}.

Two works are sufficiently close to the present study to warrant more precise comparison.
McRae \emph{et al.}~\cite{mcrae2021} argue that coherence metrics cannot yet be reliably
compared across measurements, devices, and laboratories because temporal fluctuations, TLS
dynamics, and incompletely reported experimental conditions can make the claimed differences
unreliable. Their concern is experimental reproducibility. The present paper develops a modeling
analog of that argument: a comparison extracted from a swept design space is likewise
unreliable unless both the controlled variable and the figure of merit are specified alongside
the reported value.

Lange \emph{et al.}~\cite{lange2025} directly compare transmon and fluxonium lattices and
conclude that fluxonium architectures may show lower cross-talk when designed for similar gate
speeds and fidelities, a matching they argue from coupling strengths in published
two-qubit-gate experiments. Their figures of merit are residual $ZZ$ cross-talk and
state localization rather than coherence. Our work is complementary: it applies the same
principle of controlled comparison to coherence and asks how the conclusion changes when
transition frequency is held fixed.

\subsection{Contribution}

Our contribution is not the frequency scaling itself, but its consequence for how design studies
are interpreted:

\begin{enumerate}
\item We quantify the frequency confound in both architectures, finding $|r|>0.98$ between modeled $\log T_2$ and $\log f_{01}$ across the relevant design planes. As a result, a raw
      $T_2$ optimum can primarily reflect the lowest-frequency boundary of the sweep rather than
      an intrinsic design optimum.
\item We show that the transmon's anharmonicity-based gate-count optimum within the 3--6\,GHz band is pinned to the
      imposed regime threshold across eight thresholds spanning $E_J/E_C=15$--$60$. The
      substantive choice is therefore the threshold itself, rather than the subsequent
      optimization within that domain.
\item We show that including second-order $1/f$ flux dephasing produces an interior fluxonium
      optimum that is absent from a first-order treatment.
\item We show that the transmon--fluxonium coherence comparison varies substantially across
      defensible figures of merit, and we estimate the gate-count comparison, the one most
      sensitive to its assumptions, at 17--27$\times$ with a lab-frame pulse simulation.
\end{enumerate}

We are not aware of prior work that explicitly examines how strongly the transmon--fluxonium
coherence comparison depends on the chosen metric. This rests on the survey above, which is
not exhaustive; the individual ingredients are known, and the claim concerns their combination.
Our contribution is to show this dependence
and how controlling for transition frequency changes the resulting design conclusions.

\subsection{A limitation of the present comparison}
\label{sec:matched-choice}

Matching $f_{01}$ removes the dominant confound but not all of it. Dielectric loss also depends
on geometry and surface participation, which differ between architectures and which a
Hamiltonian-level model does not capture~\cite{wang2015}, and real devices fluctuate in ways no
static model represents~\cite{mcrae2021}. Frequency matching is necessary to separate architecture-dependent effects from the
transition-frequency difference, but not sufficient for a complete hardware comparison;
comparing devices at their intended operating points answers a different, also legitimate,
question. Our matched-frequency numbers should be read as removing one large confound rather
than as a fully controlled experiment.

A second limitation follows from the matching itself. The fluxonium's sweet-spot frequency lies
below the transmon band, so the matched transmons sit at 0.5--2\,GHz with
$E_J/E_C = 325$--$5050$ and $|\alpha| \approx \SI{10}{\mega\hertz}$, far outside the design
envelope adopted in Section~\ref{sec:transmon}. The matched transmon at each frequency is the one with the highest $T_2$ allowed by
$E_J/E_C\ge50$, and since modeled $T_2$ rises slowly with $E_J/E_C$ that is the most favorable
transmon the scan contains, at the edge of its $E_C$ range. The choice barely matters for $T_2$:
holding the matched transmons at $E_J/E_C=50$ instead changes their $T_2$ by at most 1.3\% and the
1\,GHz Monte Carlo median of Section~\ref{sec:q3} from $0.37\times$ to $0.38\times$. It does mean
the matched transmons lie outside the adopted design envelope and are unsuited to the assumed
gate speeds, and gate-based metrics at matched frequency should not be read as device
comparisons.

A third limitation is that the two noise models are not symmetric. Quasiparticle tunneling is
modeled for the fluxonium but not for the transmon, for which \texttt{scqubits} provides no such
channel; inductive loss in the fluxonium's superinductance, a known loss channel in real devices,
is omitted; and only the fluxonium model is calibrated against measurement
(Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). The quasiparticle asymmetry is not harmless. Evaluating the same
\texttt{scqubits} quasiparticle spectral density with the transmon's
$|\langle 0|\sin(\varphi/2)|1\rangle|^2$ gives $T_1^{\rm qp}\approx\SI{5.6}{\micro\second}$ at the
transmon operating point for the default $x_{qp}=3\times10^{-6}$, shorter than its dielectric
$T_1$ of \SI{32}{\micro\second}; at $x_{qp}=10^{-8}$ it is \SI{1.7}{\milli\second}, a 2\%
correction. The leading-order analytic transmon result of Catelani \emph{et al.}~\cite{catelani2011}
gives \SI{5.55}{\micro\second} at the same density, within 1.5\%. Quasiparticle tunneling changes charge parity, and the extended-phase matrix element used here
neglects the difference between the two parity sectors. That is an approximation: at
$E_J/E_C\ge20$ parity-dependent spectral differences are exponentially suppressed, which is why the
two estimates agree, but it does not make every effect of quasiparticle tunneling identical in the
two sectors. For the modeled quasiparticle-induced $0\to1$ relaxation, omitting the channel is
approximately equivalent to taking a small transmon quasiparticle density, consistent with the
bound Nguyen \emph{et al.}\ infer for fluxonium. It does not exclude other quasiparticle- or
charge-parity-related errors~\cite{papic2024}. The fluxonium, meanwhile, carries the $300\times$ higher default. That default is irrelevant at the flux sweet
spot, where the fluxonium term vanishes, but our transmon numbers are conditional on a low
$x_{qp}$. The other two asymmetries can bias the cross-architecture ratios, in opposite
directions, by amounts we do not quantify.

\section{Model and Conventions}

\subsection{Noise channels}
\label{sec:model}

Coherence is estimated using \texttt{scqubits}~\cite{groszkowski2021}. Independent relaxation and
dephasing rates are combined as
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{2T_1} + \sum_k \frac{1}{T_{\phi,k}},
\label{eq:rates}
\end{equation}
with noise channels selected separately for each architecture. The fixed-frequency transmon
considered here contains no flux-tunable loop, so $1/f$ flux dephasing is not included. In the
fluxonium model considered here, the inductive shunt removes the dependence on offset charge
entirely, so charge dephasing is not included. Table~\ref{tab:channels} summarizes the channel
assignments.

Throughout, $E_J$, $E_C$ and $E_L$ are quoted as frequencies ($E/h$, in GHz), following
\texttt{scqubits}, and the anharmonicity is $\alpha=f_{12}-f_{01}$. $T_1$ is the inverse of the total
relaxation rate, the sum of the downward rate and the thermally activated upward rate
($\Gamma_\downarrow+\Gamma_\uparrow$), which is the \texttt{scqubits} default. Each $1/f$ channel has spectral density $S_\lambda(\omega)=2\pi A_\lambda^2/|\omega|$ in the noise
coordinate $\lambda$, the \texttt{scqubits} convention, which Nguyen \emph{et al.}\ also use for
Eq.~\eqref{eq:secondorder}; we use the package's default cutoffs and measurement time, and the first-order dephasing rate depends
explicitly only on the infrared cutoff $\omega_{\rm low}=2\pi\times\SI{1}{\hertz}$ and the measurement time
$t_{\rm exp}=\SI{10}{\micro\second}$. $A_\Phi$ is in units of $\Phi_0$. $A_{n_g}=10^{-4}$ is defined as an amplitude in the dimensionless coordinate $n_g$, which counts
offset charge in units of the Cooper-pair charge $2e$; it corresponds to $2\times10^{-4}e$. This is
Koch \emph{et al.}'s convention: their worked example uses $A=10^{-4}$ with $\partial E_{01}/\partial n_g$
in Eq.~(5.9)~\cite{koch2007}, and Section~\ref{sec:validation} reproduces it. It is also what
\texttt{scqubits} computes, although the package labels the default as in units of $e$. Critical-current noise is where we depart from the package. \texttt{scqubits} documents $A_{\rm cc}$ in units of
the critical current, but applies it to the dimensionless operator $\partial H/\partial E_J=-\cos\varphi$,
so the amplitude acts as an absolute fluctuation of $E_J$ in GHz. We read $A_{\rm cc}$ as the fractional fluctuation $\epsilon_I=\delta I_c/I_c$, as the literature
amplitudes it stands for are: since $E_J=\hbar I_c/2e$, the perturbation is
$\delta H(t)=-E_J\cos\hat\varphi\,\epsilon_I(t)$ with $S_{\epsilon_I}(\omega)=2\pi A_{\rm cc}^2/|\omega|$, and we
pass $A_{\rm cc}E_J$ to the package. The two readings differ by $E_J/h$ in GHz: $8.9\times$ for the nominal fluxonium and
$13\times$ for the transmon operating point. Under the absolute reading the critical-current rate of a transmon at fixed $f_{01}$ would also
depend on $E_J/E_C$: since $\partial f_{01}/\partial E_J\approx\sqrt{2E_C/E_J}$, it would fall as
$(E_J/E_C)^{-1/2}$, the dephasing time growing as $\sqrt{E_J/E_C}$. A fractional $E_J$ fluctuation
instead shifts $f_{01}$ by $E_J\,\partial f_{01}/\partial E_J\approx(f_{01}+E_C)/2$ at any $E_J/E_C$.
The choice matters. Under the absolute reading, which the package applies by default, the nominal
fluxonium $T_2$ would be \SI{1130}{\micro\second} rather than \SI{369}{\micro\second}, the raw
fluxonium/transmon ratio $18.1\times$ rather than $8.4\times$, the matched-frequency ratios
1.07--2.16$\times$ rather than 0.62--0.82$\times$, and the calibration of Section~\ref{sec:calibration}
$2.6\times$ optimistic on $T_2$ rather than $1.5\times$. Critical-current dephasing carries a median 76\% of the fluxonium's total decoherence rate over its design plane, so its amplitude is the dominant uncertainty for that architecture. Koch \emph{et al.}\ use the same fractional amplitude, $\bar A=A/I_c$, independent of
$E_J$~\cite{koch2007}. The convention and the value are separate questions. Measured $1/f$
critical-current noise in Al/AlOx and Nb-based junctions~\cite{vanharlingen2004,eroms2006,nugroho2013},
extrapolated to 15--20\,mK and junction areas of 0.01--0.1\,$\upmu$m$^2$, gives $\sqrt{S_{\delta I_c/I_c}(1\,{\rm Hz})}\approx1.4\times10^{-7}$--$1.4\times10^{-6}\,{\rm Hz}^{-1/2}$,
clustering near $2$--$6\times10^{-7}\,{\rm Hz}^{-1/2}$. For a $1/f$ spectrum this square root at 1\,Hz is
the dimensionless coefficient $A_{\rm cc}$ of our convention, up to the factor $\sqrt2$ between one-
and two-sided spectra, which the measurements do not specify; the temperature scaling below ${\sim}0.3$\,K is not measured, and the
extrapolations disagree by about $2.5\times$. Our baseline $A_{\rm cc}=10^{-7}$ therefore lies near the optimistic end of the range extrapolated from
published measurements, Koch \emph{et al.}\ use $10^{-6}$, and the Monte Carlo range of
Section~\ref{sec:q3}, $10^{-7}$--$10^{-6}$, spans the extrapolations. The adopted amplitude is a literature-motivated modeling assumption, not a
device-specific measurement.

Equation~\eqref{eq:rates} is an approximation: we represent each relaxation and dephasing
contribution by an effective exponential rate and add the rates to define a comparative $T_2$.
Independence of the noise processes, exponential decay and additivity are separate assumptions,
and the construction does not in general reproduce the decay envelope. Linear coupling to quasistatic $1/f$ noise instead produces approximately Gaussian
dephasing, and at a sweet spot, where the coupling is quadratic, the accumulated phase is
quadratic in the noise and the envelope differs from both forms. The first- and second-order flux
terms also arise from the same noise process, so adding their rates is a further approximation,
most relevant at small detunings where the two are comparable. Equation~\eqref{eq:rates}
therefore summarizes a non-exponential decay by a single effective time. We use it, as is
standard in design studies, to compare designs rather than to predict measured decay curves.

We use $Q_{\mathrm{cap}}=10^6$, $A_{n_g}=10^{-4}$, $A_\Phi=10^{-6}\,\Phi_0$, and
$A_{\mathrm{cc}}=10^{-7}$. The chosen flux-noise amplitude, $A_\Phi=1\,\upmu\Phi_0$, is slightly below
the values measured on flux-sensitive devices, $1.4\,\upmu\Phi_0$~\cite{yan2016} and
$1.8\,\upmu\Phi_0$~\cite{nguyen2019}, and is therefore optimistic. Temperature, quasiparticle density, and the $1/f$ cutoffs are assumed parameters, taken from the
\texttt{scqubits} defaults ($T=\SI{15}{\milli\kelvin}$,
$x_{\mathrm{qp}}=3\times10^{-6}$, $t_{\mathrm{exp}}=\SI{10}{\micro\second}$) and are recorded with
every result because these parameters materially affect the predicted coherence times.

\subsection{Second-order flux dephasing}

\texttt{scqubits} evaluates $1/f$ dephasing to first order in the sensitivity
$\partial\omega_{01}/\partial\lambda$. At an exact flux sweet spot,
$\partial\omega_{01}/\partial\Phi=0$, so the first-order dephasing rate vanishes and the
corresponding calculated dephasing time diverges~\cite{groszkowski2021}. The leading flux
sensitivity at half flux is therefore second order. We include the second-order contribution
using the expression employed for fluxonium by Nguyen \emph{et al.}~\cite{nguyen2019}, following
Ithier \emph{et al.}~\cite{ithier2005}:
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_\lambda^{(2)} = \left|\frac{\partial^2\omega_{01}}{\partial\lambda^2}\right| A_\lambda^2 ,
\label{eq:secondorder}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the dimensionless noise coordinate ($\lambda=\Phi/\Phi_0$ for flux, $\lambda=n_g$
for charge), so the curvature is in rad\,s$^{-1}$ and $A_\lambda$ is dimensionless: $A_\lambda=10^{-6}$
for flux is $A_\Phi=1\,\upmu\Phi_0$. The absolute value keeps the rate positive at a sweet spot of either curvature.
Equation~\eqref{eq:secondorder} contains no logarithmic factor under this convention, and it is a
phenomenological effective rate rather than a derivation of the decay envelope. Alternative
treatments introduce factors involving $|\ln(\omega_{\mathrm{low}}t_{\mathrm{exp}})|$, producing
substantial quantitative changes in the inferred dephasing time. Rather than selecting among these
conventions implicitly, we report the associated sensitivity explicitly
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The transmon at $n_g=0$ is also a first-order sweet spot, for
charge, so the same divergence occurs there. The analogous second-order charge term, evaluated
with Eq.~\eqref{eq:secondorder} at $\lambda=n_g$ and $A_{n_g}$, gives $T_\phi\ge\SI{0.15}{\second}$ everywhere with
$E_J/E_C\ge20$ (\SI{78}{\second} at the operating point), and we omit it as negligible. That estimate uses the same convention as the flux term but, unlike
it, has not been checked against an independent published number. It is negligible throughout the adopted $E_J/E_C\ge20$ regime under our assumed noise model, and we
do not rely on that omission when interpreting behavior below the regime boundary. The required curvature is evaluated using Richardson
extrapolation, since a simple central difference remains 4--10\% from its converged value in the
deepest-well part of the scan ($E_J=40$--\SI{45}{\giga\hertz}, $E_L=\SI{0.2}{\giga\hertz}$),
though the finite-difference estimates agree to within 0.2\% under the step-size extrapolation at
the interior optima of Section~\ref{sec:fluxonium}.

\subsection{A declared modeling choice}
\label{sec:model-choice}

Passing $Q_{\mathrm{cap}}$ explicitly replaces the \texttt{scqubits} default frequency-dependent
quality factor, $Q_{\mathrm{cap}}(\omega)\propto\omega^{-0.7}$~\cite{smith2020}, with a constant
value. At the fluxonium sweet spot, these two assumptions change the calculated $T_1$ by a factor
of $7.1$, making this the largest single modeling choice in the study. The resulting uncertainty
is not symmetric: Nguyen \emph{et al.} fit their measured $T_1$-versus-frequency dependence with
$\tan\delta_C\propto\omega^{0.15}$, and at $f_{01}=\SI{0.363}{\giga\hertz}$ the constant-$Q$ model
lies $1.52\times$ from their measured scaling, compared with $4.7\times$ for the \texttt{scqubits}
default. That exponent was fit jointly with thermal effects and is not an independent measurement
of the loss tangent, but it favors the constant-$Q_{\mathrm{cap}}$ model, which we therefore retain as the baseline because it is closer to the fitted frequency scaling of
those devices over the range examined here, while stating the assumption explicitly. The frequency confound of Sections~\ref{sec:transmon} and~\ref{sec:fluxonium} does
not depend on this choice. Under the frequency-dependent default,
$\mathrm{corr}(\log T_2,\log f_{01})$ is $-0.9997$ for the transmon ($E_J/E_C\ge20$) and $-0.9718$
for the fluxonium, against $-0.9999$ and $-0.9878$ here, and both raw maxima remain at the
lowest-frequency grid point. The cross-architecture comparison does depend on it. Under the
default, the raw $T_2$ ratio of Section~\ref{sec:q3} rises from $8.4\times$ to $9.6\times$, and
the deterministic matched-frequency ratios fall from $0.82$, $0.62$ and $0.62\times$ at 0.5, 1 and
2\,GHz to $0.37$, $0.37$ and $0.48\times$: the longer dielectric $T_1$ leaves the fluxonium's
critical-current dephasing a larger share of its rate budget (91\% rather than 75\% at 1\,GHz). The
size of the matched-frequency ratio therefore depends on the loss model, although under both it
favors the transmon.

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

Forty-four checks of the numerical pipeline run before the results presented below are generated.
They are numerical checks, analytic benchmarks and selected experimental comparisons, and they test
the numerics and the stated limits rather than the completeness of the noise model. They include exact Hamiltonian symmetries to machine precision, basis-size
convergence, analytic limits from Koch \emph{et al.}~\cite{koch2007}, and, in several cases,
comparison with published data from experimental devices.

The strongest analytic benchmark is Koch \emph{et al.}'s worked charge-dephasing example. For
$E_J=\SI{30}{\giga\hertz}$, $E_C=\SI{0.35}{\giga\hertz}$, and $A=10^{-4}$, they report
$T_2\sim\SI{8}{\second}$. Substituting our computed charge dispersion into their Eq.~(5.9) gives
$T_2=\SI{8.75}{\second}$. At $E_J/E_C=50$, the maximum charge-dispersion derivative is $0.830$
times their asymptotic value of $1.5\times10^{-4}E_C$, consistent with the asymptotic expression
being approached from below.

Against Manucharyan \emph{et al.}~\cite{manucharyan2009}, the lowest two transitions at
$\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}=0$ are reproduced to better than $0.7\%$ for each of the paper's three
published parameter sets. A single four-parameter fit additionally reproduces all six digitized
transition lines across two flux biases with an RMS error of $\SI{0.0102}{\giga\hertz}$. The three
published parameter sets---the rounded main-text values and two Table~I least-squares fits---produce
a $9.5\%$ spread in $f_{01}$ at the flux sweet spot. Agreement quoted only against the rounded
main-text parameters would therefore overstate the experimental constraint.

The third transition at $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}=0$ is excluded from this comparison for a physical
rather than numerical reason. Its bare frequency lies only 0.14--0.36\,GHz from the junction-array
mode placed at $\SI{10.78}{\giga\hertz}$ by Manucharyan's extended model, while the 0--3 coupling,
which we compute from that model, is approximately $\SI{0.53}{\giga\hertz}$. The coupling therefore
exceeds the detuning by a factor of 1.5--3.7, placing the transition in a strongly hybridized regime
that cannot be captured by the bare-circuit model. Explicit diagonalization of the fluxonium coupled
to both array modes confirms that this discrepancy is concentrated near $\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}=0$. The
dressed $f_{01}$ shifts by $-16\%$ there, but by only $-0.43\%$ at the flux sweet spot, where the
coherence calculations in this study are performed. This residual shift is smaller than the $9.5\%$
spread produced by the paper's own published parameter sets.

Finally, the second-order channel in Eq.~\eqref{eq:secondorder} is the only physical contribution
implemented here rather than inherited from established tooling, so we check it against an
independent published evaluation rather than against its defining equation alone. Nguyen
\emph{et al.}, using the same expression and their measured $A_\Phi=1.8\,\upmu\Phi_0$, state that
the second-order limit exceeds $\SI{10}{\milli\second}$ for all of their qubits~\cite{nguyen2019}.
Evaluated for their nine devices at the same amplitude, our implementation gives $T_{\phi}^{(2)}=15$--$\SI{804}{\milli\second}$, consistent with that statement.
This tests the rate expression in the parameter regime of the published devices; it does not
independently validate its extrapolation to the much deeper-well designs of
Section~\ref{sec:fluxonium}.

\section{The Transmon Design Plane}
\label{sec:transmon}

We sweep $E_J \in [1, 80]$, $E_C \in [0.1, 2.0]$\,GHz at $n_g = 0$ and $n_g = 0.25$
(800 points each), and refine to a $65\times53$ grid over $E_J \in [4,20]$,
$E_C \in [0.08, 0.6]$\,GHz.

\subsection{The confound}

Within the transmon regime $E_J/E_C \ge 20$, $\mathrm{corr}(\log T_2, \log f_{01}) = -0.9999$.
Under this noise model the coherence landscape over the plane is effectively one-dimensional:
$E_J$ and $E_C$ remain independent design variables, but $T_2$ depends on them almost only through
$f_{01}$. The reason is structural rather than numerical: dielectric loss dominates $T_1$
everywhere in the regime, and at transmon frequencies $hf_{01}\gg k_BT$, with
$\Gamma \propto |\langle 0|\hat n|1\rangle|^2 S(\omega_{01})$. Since
$|\langle 0|\hat n|1\rangle|^2 \propto \sqrt{E_J/E_C}$ and $S \propto E_C$, the rate goes as
$\sqrt{E_JE_C} \propto \omega_{01}$---a function of frequency alone. The critical-current rate,
set by $E_J\,\partial\omega_{01}/\partial E_J\approx\omega_{01}/2$, is too. Consequently the best sampled $T_2$ point of the swept plane, \SI{147}{\micro\second} at $E_J = 3.03$,
$E_C = \SI{0.10}{\giga\hertz}$, sits at the corner of the sampled domain---the smallest $E_C$ on the grid and the smallest
sampled $E_J$ satisfying $E_J/E_C \ge 20$ (the $E_J$ grid spacing is \SI{2.03}{\giga\hertz}, so the
exact boundary $E_J=\SI{2.0}{\giga\hertz}$ is not sampled)---at
$f_{01} = \SI{1.45}{\giga\hertz}$,
below the conventional \SIrange{3}{6}{\giga\hertz} transmon operating band~\cite{krantz2019}. It is a
frequency minimum, not a design.

\subsection{No interior optimum in this model}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/transmon_tradeoff.pdf}
\caption{The transmon trade-off at fixed frequency ($f_{01} = 5.00 \pm
\SI{0.05}{\giga\hertz}$). (a) The anharmonicity-based gate-count estimate falls monotonically with $E_J/E_C$; the three
vertical lines are candidate regime thresholds, and the optimum sits on whichever one is
imposed. (b) $T_2$ is flat to 3.2\% while $|\alpha|$ falls $7.3\times$; the shaded band is
the Krantz \SIrange{100}{300}{\mega\hertz} design window. Charge insensitivity costs anharmonicity, not coherence.}
\label{fig:tradeoff}
\end{figure}

Holding $f_{01} = 5.00 \pm \SI{0.05}{\giga\hertz}$ and sweeping $E_J/E_C$ from 11.4 to 117.6, a cut
that extends below the $E_J/E_C\ge20$ regime to show the trend,
$T_2$ is flat to 3.2\% (\SIrange{42.64}{44.02}{\micro\second}) while $|\alpha|$ falls by a
factor of 7.3 (\SIrange{1353}{185}{\mega\hertz}). \textbf{Charge insensitivity is bought with anharmonicity, and essentially not with coherence at
all} (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). Lower anharmonicity tightens the leakage constraint on pulse design,
but how much it lengthens a real gate depends on pulse shaping, matrix elements and the error target,
as the pulse simulation of Section~\ref{sec:pulse} shows.

That the trade is anharmonicity-for-charge-insensitivity is expected from Koch's asymptotics:
with $\alpha \simeq -E_C$ and $f_{01} \simeq \sqrt{8E_JE_C}$ held fixed, raising $E_J/E_C$
forces $E_C \propto (E_J/E_C)^{-1/2}$, predicting a factor 3.21 over our swept range against a
computed $E_C$ ratio of 3.47. The observed $7.3\times$ fall in $|\alpha|$ is steeper still
because $\alpha \simeq -E_C$ is asymptotic and fails at the low end: $|\alpha|/E_C = 2.29$ at
$E_J/E_C = 11.4$ against $1.09$ at $117.6$. The trade-off is therefore harshest exactly in the
low-$E_J/E_C$ corner that an unconstrained gate-count metric wants to select, which is an
independent reason to keep the design envelope.

Relocating the gate-count optimum under eight regime thresholds spanning $E_J/E_C = 15$ to
60, the optimum tracks every one of them to within 0.24. It sits \emph{on} the constraint,
never inside the domain. This is the signature of a constrained optimum, and it means the
substantive choice in this part of the design space is the threshold, not the optimization. The correct claim is a monotone trade-off terminated wherever the
regime boundary is drawn, and any quoted optimum is meaningful only together with its
threshold. We adopt the conventional design envelope of Krantz \emph{et al.} ($E_J/E_C \ge 50$, $|\alpha| \in
[100,300]$\,MHz, $f_{01} \in [3,6]$\,GHz)~\cite{krantz2019} because it is independent of every
noise amplitude in the study, giving the operating point $E_J = \SI{13.0}{\giga\hertz}$,
$E_C = \SI{0.26}{\giga\hertz}$, $f_{01} = \SI{4.92}{\giga\hertz}$,
$T_2 = \SI{44.1}{\micro\second}$, $|\alpha| = \SI{299}{\mega\hertz}$.

A limitation of the model bears on this choice: \texttt{tphi\_1\_over\_f\_ng} covers $1/f$
dephasing only, and the broadband charge sensitivity that motivates Krantz's
$E_J/E_C \ge 50$ is absent. Left to itself the metric under-penalizes low $E_J/E_C$ and would
recommend $|\alpha| \approx \SI{728}{\mega\hertz}$, roughly $2.4\times$ what is typically fabricated.
The envelope is a constraint imposed on the model, not a result derived from it.

\section{The Fluxonium Design Plane}
\label{sec:fluxonium}

Over $E_J \in [3,15]$, $E_L \in [0.2,1.5]$\,GHz at $E_C = \SI{2.5}{\giga\hertz}$ and
$\Phi/\Phi_0 = 1/2$, the same confound appears: $\mathrm{corr}(\log T_2, \log f_{01}) = -0.9878$. It is looser than for
the transmon. About a log-log fit (slope $-0.79$), fluxonium $T_2$ at fixed $f_{01}$ still scatters by
12\% rms, and by up to 39\% at the worst point, against 0.6\% rms for the transmon; frequency is the
dominant variable here, not the only one. The unconstrained maximum, \SI{1398}{\micro\second}, sits at both grid edges at
$f_{01} = \SI{75}{\mega\hertz}$, where $\bar n_{\rm th} = 3.7$, and $2.3\times$ below the
lowest-frequency device in Nguyen \emph{et al.}'s Table~I. Constrained to that table's range,
\SIrange{0.17}{0.83}{\giga\hertz}, the best sampled design gives \SI{719}{\micro\second} at $E_J = 10.6$, $E_L = \SI{0.20}{\giga\hertz}$
($f_{01}=\SI{0.177}{\giga\hertz}$), itself on the $E_L$ grid edge and at the low end of the band: the
confound again.

Here and below, $\bar n_{\rm th} = 1/(e^{hf_{01}/k_BT}-1)$ is the Bose occupation of the bath at
the qubit frequency, at the model's $T = \SI{15}{\milli\kelvin}$. It is not the qubit's own
population. The qubit's equilibrium excited-state population, $p_1 = 1/(1+e^{hf_{01}/k_BT})$, is
44\% at \SI{75}{\mega\hertz}, but it is already 24\% for the nominal design at
\SI{0.363}{\giga\hertz}: thermal population is a matter of degree across this whole regime, and
under these conditions passive thermal equilibrium does not provide high-fidelity ground-state
initialization for any fluxonium here; active reset, heralding or engineered cooling is needed. Effective qubit temperatures in real devices
are often above the bath temperature assumed here.

\subsection{An interior optimum, created by second-order dephasing}

Extending the scan to $E_J = \SI{45}{\giga\hertz}$ reveals a one-dimensional interior maximum of $T_2$ in $E_J$ within this model, present in eight of
the nine $(E_C, E_L)$ slices tested; this is not a joint optimum over all three parameters, and in the ninth ($E_C=3.5$, $E_L=\SI{0.2}{\giga\hertz}$) $T_2$ is still rising at the edge of
the scan, so its optimum is not located (Fig.~\ref{fig:interior}). It is created by the second-order
flux channel: as $E_J$ rises and $E_L$ falls the qubit sinks into a deep double well,
$f_{01}$ collapses, and $\partial^2 f_{01}/\partial\Phi^2$ grows by three orders of
magnitude across the scan, so $\Gamma_\Phi^{(2)}$ grows with it and eventually overwhelms the improving dielectric and
critical-current terms. Located by continuous optimization in $E_J$ at each of the nine
$(E_C, E_L)$ slices rather than read off the $\Delta E_J = 2.5$\,GHz grid, the optimum moves by at most \SI{0.9}{\giga\hertz} in $E_J$ and 0.8\% in $T_2$, and at it the
second-order channel carries 46--47\% of the total decoherence rate $1/T_2$ in the eight located
slices. That is what an interior optimum of this kind looks like: the channel that turns the curve over is
comparable to everything else at the turn. It is an optimum of this model. Its location inherits the
uncertainty in the second-order rate convention (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), the loss model and the
omitted channels, and it applies to the slices searched. Two of those dependences are large. Under
a $\ln^2$ second-order convention the optimum persists but moves up by an order of magnitude in
frequency, to 0.18--\SI{0.31}{\giga\hertz} in the two slices checked; and the array-mode bound of
Section~\ref{sec:validation} does not extend to these deep-well designs. A first-order-only
pipeline shows no turnover over the same range and rises monotonically, reaching $5.7\times10^{4}$\,\si{\micro\second} at the end of the scan (a model output, not an achievable
coherence)---which is precisely how an earlier version of this
analysis concluded there was no interior optimum, and is a concrete argument for including the
second-order channel in any design study that sweeps toward low $E_L$.

The mechanism is geometric. As $E_J$ rises and $E_L$ falls the potential minima separate and
the tunnel splitting that sets $f_{01}$ collapses; the same deepening well sharpens the
curvature to which $\Gamma_\Phi^{(2)}$ is proportional by Eq.~\eqref{eq:secondorder}, so a
single geometric change both lowers the frequency and raises the second-order rate. The
investigated transmon model shows no analogous turnover: its charge dispersion falls exponentially
in $\sqrt{E_J/E_C}$ with no turning point.

The optimum nevertheless carries a cost. In the convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:secondorder} it occurs at $f_{01} = 5.4$--\SI{30}{\mega\hertz}, with $\bar n_{\rm th} = 10$--57 and an equilibrium
excited-state population of 48--50\%, so passive thermal equilibration at \SI{15}{\milli\kelvin} does not provide high-fidelity
ground-state initialization for these designs. That is a cost, not a barrier: Zhang \emph{et al.} operate a \SI{14}{\mega\hertz} fluxonium, initialize it with 97\% fidelity and
run single-qubit gates with 99.8\% average fidelity~\cite{zhang2021}, and Najera-Santos \emph{et al.} cool a \SI{1.8}{\mega\hertz} fluxonium to
a 97.7\% ground-state population~\cite{najerasantos2024}. Under the 15\,mK equilibrium assumed here the
optimum is thermally unfavorable rather than impossible; whether it is worth reaching is a
question of initialization overhead, which this model does not include, and these results do
not show that the particular designs found here would work experimentally. Past the turnover the frequency correlation reverses sign: within each slice of
Fig.~\ref{fig:interior}, $\mathrm{corr}(\log T_2,\log f_{01})$ is $+0.98$ to $+0.99$ beyond the optimum,
against $-0.99$ over the in-band plane. The second-order channel adds new structure rather than
rescaling the old.

\section{Dominant Noise Channels}
\label{sec:channels}

At a charge-sensitive bias ($n_g = 0.25$) charge noise dominates the transmon below
$E_J/E_C \approx 25$ and dielectric relaxation above it. That crossover is computed from our numerical sweep and lands close to the lower edge of the transmon
window of Koch \emph{et al.}, $20\lesssim E_J/E_C\ll5\times10^4$~\cite{koch2007}. The two are not
independent: both reflect the exponential suppression of charge dispersion, and Koch \emph{et
al.}'s pulse-duration argument sets only the upper end of their window, through the shrinking
anharmonicity. The agreement is therefore a consistency check, and it does not by itself validate the
noise amplitudes or the predicted coherence times. Critical-current noise never dominates the transmon anywhere in its 1600 points at literature
amplitudes, though it carries about a third of the rate at the operating point. The crossover is noise-model dependent (it spans 20.8--29.9
across literature ranges) and is therefore reported as a model result, not a universal threshold, rather than used as an
input.

For the fluxonium the picture is different. Critical-current dephasing dominates at the sweet
spot, where it carries 76\% of $1/T_2$, and at 358 of the 400 points of the design plane, and
first-order flux noise becomes the largest single term only beyond $8.7\times10^{-5}\,\Phi_0$. That
makes the fluxonium results sensitive above all to $A_{\rm cc}$ and its convention (Section~\ref{sec:model}). Quasiparticle tunneling is not a
dead channel: it is suppressed only \emph{at} the sweet spot, where its $0$--$1$ matrix
element vanishes by symmetry, and limits $T_1$ at most detuned points. This finding is
conditional on $x_{qp} = 3\times10^{-6}$, a default roughly $300\times$ above the bound of
$10^{-8}$ that Nguyen \emph{et al.} infer for their devices; at their density the channel is negligible everywhere. It is one of the most fragile results in the study, after the critical-current dependence above,
and is reported as conditional.

\section{The Cross-Architecture Comparison}
\label{sec:q3}

The comparison uses the transmon operating point of Section~\ref{sec:transmon}
($E_J = \SI{13.0}{\giga\hertz}$, $E_C = \SI{0.26}{\giga\hertz}$) and the nominal fluxonium
$E_J = \SI{8.9}{\giga\hertz}$, $E_C = \SI{2.5}{\giga\hertz}$, $E_L = \SI{0.5}{\giga\hertz}$ at
half flux ($f_{01} = \SI{0.363}{\giga\hertz}$, $T_2 = \SI{368.8}{\micro\second}$). The fluxonium
is chosen because it is the spectroscopically validated device of Section~\ref{sec:validation},
not the best design in band: the best Table~I-band design of Section~\ref{sec:fluxonium} would raise the raw ratio by a further $1.9\times$, mostly by lowering the frequency.

\subsection{Bias robustness, the one unambiguous asymmetry}

Both bias coordinates are periodic with period one, so the width of the window over which
$T_2$ stays within a factor $k$ of its sweet-spot value can be compared as a fraction of one bias
period. This is a comparison of normalized sensitivity, not of the physical precision each bias
demands: offset charge is not normally controlled at all, which is why insensitivity to it
matters, while flux is set by a bias current. The transmon's $T_2$ varies by $1.0040\times$ across its \emph{entire}
charge period---a factor-of-two loss never occurs at any bias. The fluxonium loses a factor
of two within $\pm 1.11\times10^{-4}\,\Phi_0$ and a factor of ten within $9.96\times10^{-4}\,\Phi_0$, located by root-finding on the model rather than read off a
grid. The usable bias window is therefore at least $4.5\times10^{3}$ times wider for the transmon,
a lower bound because the transmon never reaches the twofold loss.

The falloff exponent is not a single number. It approaches the first-order $1/f$ prediction
of $-1$ only in an intermediate window where $T_2$ is genuinely flux-dephasing limited,
reaching $-0.929$ there, and flattens on both sides---capped by the $2T_1$ ceiling close in,
and contaminated by other channels far out.

Intrinsic $1/f$ flux noise does not by itself drive the qubit off the spot: at Nguyen's measured amplitude, in the two-sided convention $S_\Phi(\omega)=2\pi A_\Phi^2/|\omega|$ used
for every dephasing rate here, the variance accumulated over both frequency signs between
$f_{\rm lo}=1/t$, for an observation time $t$ of one hour, and $f_{\rm hi}=\SI{1}{\giga\hertz}$ is
$\sigma_\Phi^2=2A_\Phi^2\ln(f_{\rm hi}/f_{\rm lo})$, an RMS wander of $1.4\times10^{-5}\,\Phi_0$
(a one-sided reading of the same amplitude would give $9.7\times10^{-6}\,\Phi_0$; note that this drift
estimate uses $1/t$ as its infrared cutoff, not the $\omega_{\rm low}$ of the coherence calculation), a $73\times$ margin to the tenfold-loss detuning. Under that spectrum and those
limits, systematic drift and bias calibration may become the more restrictive constraints rather
than stochastic noise---which relocates the difficulty rather than removing it, and plausibly makes it grow with qubit
count, although a single-qubit model cannot show that. A different spectrum or a longer observation time would change the margin.

\textbf{This constraint is being engineered away, which we should say plainly.} B\'en\^atre
\emph{et al.} hold gradiometric fluxonium qubits at their sweet spot passively, reporting
stability of $3\times10^{-4}\,\Phi_0$ from target with only a 15\% reduction in echo
coherence~\cite{benatre2025}. Their achieved stability lies between our computed twofold- and tenfold-loss detunings
($1.1\times10^{-4}$ and $1.0\times10^{-3}\,\Phi_0$), and their 15\% loss is smaller than our model would
give there---unsurprisingly, since their device has different $E_L$ and hence different curvature, and
echo suppresses low-frequency drift that free induction does not. We read their result as evidence that the bias-precision cost we quantify is real,
understood, and addressable by design rather than by calibration alone. It weakens the
scalability pessimism in our conclusion without touching the arithmetic behind it.

\subsection{The raw advantage is a frequency effect}

The two devices differ in frequency by $13.6\times$. If $T_2 \propto 1/f_{01}$, the frequency ratio alone would produce $\log 13.55/\log 8.36 = 123\%$ of
the log-ratio: more than the whole raw advantage, so at fixed frequency the fluxonium would be the
less coherent of the two. This is a scaling estimate under that law, not a decomposition of causes,
and the law describes the transmon better than the fluxonium. Dielectric loss dominates the transmon and critical-current dephasing the fluxonium. For the transmon the rate scales with $\omega_{01}$ and $T_2 \propto f_{01}^{-1.00}$ is obtained from a fit to the modeled design plane; for the fluxonium the thermal factor, the matrix elements and the dominant critical-current
channel break a simple $\Gamma_1\propto\omega_{01}$ law, and the scaling, $T_2\propto f_{01}^{-0.79}$
($r=-0.988$), is an empirical property of the model rather than a derived one. Because the flux sweet spot \emph{is} a frequency
minimum, the fluxonium is structurally advantaged on any uncontrolled $T_2$ comparison, and
reporting one credits the architecture with what the frequency did.

Controlling for it, the deterministic ratio at matched $f_{01}$ is already below one, 0.62--0.82$\times$
at 0.5, 1 and 2\,GHz. Propagated across the literature noise band at matched $f_{01}=\SI{1}{\giga\hertz}$,
with shared draws, the median is $0.37\times$ and the fluxonium wins in only 4.8\% of samples (19\% with
independent draws). The 90\% bootstrap interval on that median is 0.355--0.404. These are draws of the noise
parameters under our priors, not a population of fabricated devices. \textbf{Under these noise
priors, and at matched frequencies where the transmon is not a practical design, the transmon comes
out ahead in 95\% of samples.} This is the most important
negative result here: in this model no coherence advantage remains once frequency is controlled.

\subsection{Estimating gate durations by simulation}
\label{sec:pulse}

The gate-count range is wide enough to make the metric almost uninformative, so we estimate it
by simulation rather than by argument. We integrate the lab-frame Schr\"odinger equation for
each architecture with no rotating-wave approximation, using a six-level truncation of the
\texttt{scqubits} eigenbasis and a charge drive, and numerically optimize the pulse amplitude, DRAG coefficient~\cite{motzoi2009} and Stark-shifted
drive frequency at every duration; the average gate error is evaluated on the computational
subspace so that leakage is counted; Fig.~\ref{fig:pulse} shows the resulting error curves.

This exposes an effect that the anharmonicity argument misses entirely. The fluxonium's
$|\alpha|/f_{01} = 24$ looks decisive, but its drive matrix elements are unfavourable: at
half flux $|\langle 1|\hat n|2\rangle| / |\langle 0|\hat n|1\rangle| = 10.2$, against $1.37$
for the transmon. The drive couples ten times more strongly to the leakage transition than to
the qubit transition. That ratio is an important factor the anharmonicity alone does not
capture; leakage also depends on the pulse spectrum, detuning, drive strength and higher levels,
which is why we simulate rather than combine the two numbers by hand.

Table~\ref{tab:pulse} reports the shortest tested duration at which the calibrated gate reaches an
average error below
$10^{-3}$ for each architecture, and the resulting gate count. \emph{These durations are upper
bounds}: a three-parameter calibration over a fixed Gaussian shape is far from optimal
control, and more flexible optimal-control approaches, such as GRAPE or CRAB over a free pulse shape, could
shorten one or both gates, potentially by different amounts. The bracket also leaves out modeling choices that a different simulation could make
differently: we drive through the charge operator, whereas low-frequency fluxoniums are often
controlled through flux~\cite{zhang2021}; we use a single pulse family, omit decoherence during
the gate, and fix the error target at $10^{-3}$. The result is a single-model estimate that has
not been checked against an independent code or a measured gate. The point is not the precise
value but that, within this model, the simulated ratio falls well between the two naive
estimates.

We tested the numerics at the durations that set $T_{\rm gate}$. The pass/fail classification at the tested durations is stable across truncations after
recalibration, although individual optimized pulses are not: a pulse
calibrated on six levels does not carry over unchanged (the 3\,ns fluxonium pulse has error
$4\times10^{-2}$ when replayed on ten levels), but recalibrating on eight or ten levels changes no
pass/fail verdict at any tested duration, and tighter ODE tolerances reproduce every error. The
optimizer is not converged. With seven starting points and 400 evaluations instead of three and
140, both gates shorten, to \SI{6.0}{\nano\second} (transmon) and \SI{2.5}{\nano\second}
(fluxonium), on six or ten levels, and the ratio becomes $20\times$, inside the bracket; shorter
durations were not retested. The fluxonium gate also needs a Rabi frequency of
$\approx$\SIrange{0.35}{0.40}{\giga\hertz}, comparable to its own $f_{01}$; whether a charge
drive that strong is practical is not assessed here.

\section{Calibration Against Measurement}
\label{sec:calibration}

Run at the parameters of each of the nine devices in the published Table~I of Nguyen
\emph{et al.}, the model is optimistic by a median factor of $2.0\times$ on $T_1$ and $1.5\times$ on $T_2$, worst
case $3.2\times$ (device~F). The gap is not uniform, so it is not a single missing constant: the $T_2$
ratio runs from $0.57\times$ to $3.2\times$, and device~I, the device nearest our nominal design in
frequency (0.395 versus \SI{0.363}{\giga\hertz}), is the one on which the model is \emph{pessimistic}
on both, by $1.25\times$ on $T_1$ and $1.75\times$ on $T_2$. Under the absolute reading of $A_{\rm cc}$ the
$T_2$ median would be $2.6\times$ (Section~\ref{sec:model}). This calibration covers the fluxonium
only. We have not calibrated the transmon model against measured devices, so the
cross-architecture ratios of the previous section carry a transmon-side model bias that we do
not quantify.

Possible contributors include the flat $Q_{\rm cap} = 10^6$ against their measured
$\tan\delta_C = (2.0$--$3.6)\times10^{-6}$ at \SI{6}{\giga\hertz}; the absence of Purcell
decay, photon shot noise and inductive loss; the fact that their $T_2$ is a Hahn echo while this model computes free-induction decay, so the two are not like for like (because echo generally suppresses low-frequency dephasing, an
echo version of the model could give a longer $T_2$ and change the discrepancy; we do not quantify
that protocol dependence); and a temperature mismatch, since we run at the \SI{15}{\milli\kelvin}
default while they state \SI{20}{\milli\kelvin}. We have not quantified these contributions
separately, so the list is not a ranking.

The comparison also has a floor set by our own model rather than by the devices. Absolute
$T_2$ spans \SIrange{47}{1163}{\micro\second} under single-decade moves of the noise amplitudes, so
a factor of $1.5$ is well inside the band the model itself admits; the calibration should be read as establishing the right order of magnitude, not as a residual to be
explained away, and, because the protocols differ, not as a precise estimate of omitted loss. Device performance and loss parameters also differ across fabrication generations: Somoroff
\emph{et al.} report $T_2^* = 1.48$\,ms~\cite{somoroff2023}, well beyond the devices we calibrate
against, so a calibration to Nguyen's 2019 devices should not be assumed to predict later hardware
without re-evaluating the physical parameters.

We stress that the published table must be used. The arXiv v1 preprint lists eight devices
and omits device~I entirely---the paper's headline device, and the one closest to the design
regime studied here.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The model omits Purcell decay, photon shot noise, inductive loss in the junction array, TLS
spectral structure, crosstalk and any lattice; qubits are treated in isolation. The
superinductance is ideal, so array-mode hybridization is absent, though Section~\ref{sec:validation} bounds that omission at $-0.43\%$ in $f_{01}$ at the flux sweet
spot for the nominal and Manucharyan devices; it is not bounded for the deep-well designs of the
extended scan. Second-order dephasing uses a convention with no logarithmic factor; a $\ln^2$ convention would change sweet-spot $T_2$ by ${\sim}16\%$ instead of 0.2\% and would move,
though not remove, the interior optimum: in two slices we checked it rises by an order of magnitude in
frequency, to 0.18--0.31\,GHz, inside the range of Nguyen \emph{et al.}'s devices. The location of the
optimum is therefore the least robust number in Section~\ref{sec:fluxonium}. Absolute $T_2$ spans \SIrange{47}{1163}{\micro\second} under single-decade moves of the noise
amplitudes and should never be quoted without a band. The Monte Carlo varies amplitudes only; fabrication
spread in $E_J$, $E_C$, $E_L$ is not propagated, and the log-uniform priors are a choice. The
gate-count metric is a heuristic, not a literature formula: $N = T_2/T_{\rm gate}$ is a ratio of
timescales, not an achievable circuit depth.

Several checks that would strengthen these results have not been done, and the conclusions are
stated for this model only because of them. No result has been reproduced with an independent
implementation; everything comes from one code base built on \texttt{scqubits}. The interior
fluxonium optima are located by continuous optimization, but the transmon optimum and the in-band
fluxonium optimum are read from sampled grids rather than solved along the constraint boundaries.
The second-order curvature is computed by a single method, Richardson-extrapolated finite
differences. The Monte Carlo uses one fixed seed and priors of our choosing. The pulse simulation
uses one propagator and one pulse family, its optimizer is not converged, and shorter durations
were not retested with the stronger optimizer. Noise-model parity has not been tested: adding
quasiparticle loss to the transmon at the fluxonium's density, or inductive loss to the fluxonium,
could change the matched-frequency ranking, which Section~\ref{sec:model-choice} shows already
depends on the loss model, and Section~\ref{sec:matched-choice} on the choice of matched transmon.

\section{Conclusion}

Neither architecture has a coherence sweet spot in design space that comes without a cost. Over the swept design
planes the modeled $T_2$ is, to better than $|r| = 0.98$, largely a function of the transition
frequency (to 0.6\% rms for the transmon and 12\% for the fluxonium), so a raw $T_2$ maximum
locates the edge of the swept domain or the point where a frequency constraint was drawn. The
extended fluxonium scan departs from this: past its interior optimum, lowering the frequency
further makes $T_2$ worse.
The transmon is a monotone trade-off in which charge insensitivity is paid for in
anharmonicity, once, at design time. Within this model the fluxonium has an interior optimum, visible only once second-order flux dephasing is included, but in the convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:secondorder} it sits at 5.4--30\,MHz, where passive thermal equilibration at 15\,mK does not give high-fidelity initialization, and its location
depends strongly on the second-order rate convention; without the overhead of active initialization it too is a
trade-off terminated by a frequency floor.

For Q3, the honest output is a metric family, not a number. The fluxonium's raw $8.4\times$ coherence advantage is, under $T_2\propto 1/f_{01}$ scaling, more than
accounted for by frequency; at matched frequency the transmon is ahead deterministically at all three frequencies tested, under
both loss models (0.62--0.82$\times$ with the flat $Q_{\rm cap}$), and in 95\% of noise draws at 1\,GHz
(median $0.37\times$). These fluxonium numbers rest mainly on the critical-current amplitude, the least
constrained input in the study: read as \texttt{scqubits} applies it, the matched ratios would be
1.07--2.16$\times$ instead.
Under the tested charge-drive pulse model the fluxonium has a larger coherence-to-gate-duration
proxy, by roughly $17$--$27\times$ across the explored numerical settings. Its price is
operating-point precision: a normalized bias window at least $4.5\times10^3$ times narrower than the
transmon's, which must hold during operation, whether by active control, passive flux
locking~\cite{benatre2025} or other architectural means, rather than being fixed once at design
time. Whether that trade is worth making depends on qubit
count and calibration budget, which this single-qubit model does not quantify.

More generally, we suggest that architecture comparisons in this field should state the
metric, the control variable and the uncertainty band together. A single ratio between architectures evaluated at different operating points cannot, by itself,
isolate an architecture-dependent advantage.

\section{Outlook}

Two extensions would sharpen the results most. The gate-count metric is currently estimated with a
coarse calibration in a single model; optimal control over a free pulse shape (GRAPE or CRAB),
a flux drive, and a check against an independent code or a measured gate would tighten the $17$--$27\times$ bracket and, more interestingly, would test whether the fluxonium's
unfavourable leakage matrix element can be shaped around---the anharmonicity is genuinely
there, and a pulse that exploits it without driving $|1\rangle \to |2\rangle$ would move the
number back toward the leakage-only bound. Second, passive thermal equilibration at \SI{15}{\milli\kelvin} does not initialize the interior fluxonium
optimum we find with high fidelity, but low-frequency fluxoniums have been operated
with active initialization~\cite{zhang2021,najerasantos2024}; modeling that initialization, and
its cost in time and fidelity, would establish whether the optimum is a usable design point.

A broader point follows from the metric sensitivity. If a single architecture comparison can
span more than two orders of magnitude depending on choices that are all defensible, then comparisons
reported as single ratios carry less information than they appear to. We would encourage the
practice McRae \emph{et al.}~\cite{mcrae2021} advocate for experimental coherence
numbers---report the conditions with the number---to be extended to modeled design
comparisons: state the control variable, state the figure of merit, and give the spread.
